Introduction

Anatomy of a Swipe

What really happens in the few seconds between tapping your card and seeing "Approved"? In this module, you'll follow a payment from swipe to settlement.

What you'll learn
  • The 5 parties involved in every card transaction
  • Why authorization is a hold, not a transfer
  • How the BIN routes your payment to the right bank
  • The three acts: Authorization → Clearing → Settlement
  • How fees are split between the players
The Big Picture

Two Seconds, Six Entities, One Cup of Coffee

You tap your card for a $5 coffee. In under two seconds, a message travels from the merchant's terminal through six different entities and back. Yet no money moves — that comes later.

Think of it like ordering at a restaurant: the waiter confirms your order (authorization), the kitchen prepares it (clearing), and you pay on the way out (settlement).

Key insight: Authorization is just a promise. The actual fund transfer happens the next business day during settlement.
The Players

The Four-Party Model

Every card payment involves five key parties, connected by two processor technology layers:

Mermaid Diagram: Four-Party Model
Cardholder --> Merchant Merchant --> Acquirer Acquirer --> Acquirer Processor Acquirer Processor <--> Card Network Card Network <--> Issuer Processor Issuer Processor <--> Issuer

The acquirer serves the merchant. The issuer serves you, the cardholder. The card network (Visa, Mastercard) sits in the middle, routing messages and enforcing rules.

Act 1 of 3

Authorization: The Real-Time Approval

When you tap, the terminal sends an authorization request through the chain. The issuer checks your balance, fraud rules, and card status — then responds with "approved" or "declined."

The entire round-trip takes under 2 seconds. If approved, the issuer places a hold on your account for that amount. You see "pending" in your banking app.

Why not just move the money now? Because the final amount might change — think tips at restaurants, or partial fuel pump charges. The hold is a placeholder.

Notice how the BIN (first 6 digits of your card) determines which issuer receives the request. The network maintains a routing table mapping every BIN to a bank.

Act 2 of 3

Clearing: The End-of-Day Reconciliation

At the end of the business day, the merchant's acquirer bundles all approved transactions into a batch and sends them to the card network.

The network distributes each transaction to the correct issuer. Both sides confirm the final amounts — catching any adjustments like added tips or voided transactions.

Why batch instead of real-time? Processing millions of transactions one by one would be enormously expensive. Batching lets networks use netting — offsetting amounts owed between banks so only the net difference has to move.
Act 3 of 3

Settlement: Money Finally Moves

Usually by T+1 (next business day), the actual funds transfer between banks. But not the full $5 — fees are deducted along the way:

Your $5.00 Coffee
Interchange (to issuer) −$0.19
Network assessment (to Visa/MC) −$0.02
Acquirer fee (to processor) −$0.04
Merchant receives $4.75
Key Terms

Terms to Remember

BIN (Bank Identification Number)
The first six digits of a card number that identify the issuing bank and route the transaction.
Interchange
A fee paid by the merchant's bank to the cardholder's bank on each card transaction. Funds rewards programs.
ISO 8583
The international standard messaging format used for card transaction communications between all parties.
Settlement
The actual transfer of funds between banks, typically the next business day. This is when money really moves.
Acquirer Processor
The technology platform that routes transaction data from merchants through card networks for authorization and settlement.
