## A Credit Card With Your Name on It

Walk through any major retailer and you will see it: a sign at the register offering 15% off your purchase today if you open the store's credit card. The Target REDcard. The Amazon Prime Visa. The Costco Anywhere Card. These are <GlossaryTerm term="Co-Branded Card" definition="A credit or debit card that carries both a payment network logo (Visa, Mastercard) and a partner brand's logo, typically offering rewards or benefits tied to the partner's ecosystem.">co-branded credit cards</GlossaryTerm> — products that pair a retailer's brand with a bank's lending infrastructure and a card network's acceptance footprint.

Co-branded cards are not new. They have been a staple of retail and travel loyalty since the late 1980s. But for most of that history, launching one required a deep partnership with a large issuing bank, months (or years) of negotiation, and a customer base large enough to justify the effort. Only the biggest brands could play.

That is changing. A new category of fintech — <GlossaryTerm term="Credit Card as a Service (CCaaS)" definition="A fintech model where specialized platforms provide the infrastructure, bank partnerships, underwriting, and compliance needed for non-bank brands to launch and manage their own credit card programs via APIs.">Credit Card as a Service (CCaaS)</GlossaryTerm> — is making it possible for mid-market brands, vertical SaaS platforms, and digital-native companies to launch their own credit card programs without building a bank from scratch. To understand why this matters, we need to start at the beginning.

## A Brief History of Credit Cards

The modern credit card traces back to 1949, when businessman Frank McNamara found himself at a New York restaurant without cash. The story — likely embellished — led McNamara and his partner Ralph Schneider to create the Diners Club card in 1950. It was a charge card, not a revolving credit card: you paid your full balance at the end of each month. But it introduced the foundational idea that a third-party card could be accepted at multiple merchants.

Bank of America launched the BankAmericard in 1958 in Fresno, California — the first true revolving credit card issued by a bank. Cardholders could carry a balance and pay interest. The card went national, then international, and in 1976, BankAmericard rebranded as **Visa** to create a globally recognizable name free of any single bank's identity.

Meanwhile, a consortium of banks formed the Interbank Card Association in 1966, issuing cards under the Master Charge brand. In 1979, Master Charge rebranded as **Mastercard**, completing the two-network duopoly that still dominates card payments.

The co-branded era began in the mid-1980s. In 1986, Sears launched the Discover card — technically not a co-brand but a merchant creating its own network. The real watershed came in 1987 when American Airlines partnered with Citibank to launch the AAdvantage credit card, tying frequent-flyer miles to everyday spending. It was an enormous success. Within a few years, nearly every major airline and hotel chain had a co-branded card. Retailers followed. By 1991, co-branded cards were a category unto themselves.

Today, the US alone has approximately 1.12 billion credit cards in circulation. The average American holds four credit cards. But the market remains heavily concentrated: the six largest banks — JPMorgan Chase, Citibank, Bank of America, Capital One, American Express, and Discover — issue the vast majority of credit card balances. Co-branded partnerships have traditionally been the only path for non-bank brands to participate in this market.

## How the Co-Brand Model Works

A co-branded credit card involves three core parties working together.

The **brand** (say, Delta Air Lines) provides its name, its customer base, and its loyalty program. The brand decides what rewards the card offers — 2x miles on dining, free checked bags, priority boarding — and handles the marketing.

The **issuing bank** (say, American Express, for the Delta card) provides the balance sheet. The bank extends the credit, manages the lending risk, handles regulatory compliance, and bears the losses if cardholders default. The bank also controls the underwriting: who gets approved, at what credit limit, and at what APR.

The **payment network** (Visa, Mastercard, or American Express acting as both network and issuer) provides the acceptance infrastructure. The network's logo on the card is what lets you use it at any of the millions of merchants in its network.

The economics of the arrangement center on interchange revenue sharing. When a co-branded card is swiped, the merchant pays interchange fees — typically 1.5% to 3% of the transaction amount. The issuing bank collects that interchange, keeps a portion to cover its costs and credit risk, and shares a portion with the brand partner. This revenue share is the financial engine that funds the rewards the brand offers to cardholders.

For a large co-brand partnership like Delta and American Express, the economics are massive. American Express reportedly pays Delta roughly $7 billion per year in revenue sharing and other payments. That single credit card relationship generates more revenue for Delta than selling seats in the back of the plane.

## The Traditional Co-Brand Issuers

A handful of banks have built their entire business around issuing co-branded cards for retail and consumer brands.

**Synchrony Financial** is the largest player, controlling over 40% of the US retail co-brand and private-label credit card market. Synchrony issues cards for brands like Amazon (the Amazon Store Card), Lowe's, Sam's Club, PayPal Credit, Verizon, and dozens of others. The company manages over 70 million active accounts. Synchrony's model is simple: if you are a large retailer and you want a credit card with your name on it, Synchrony will build the program, take on the credit risk, manage the servicing, and share the interchange.

**Comenity Bank** (owned by Bread Financial) takes a similar approach but targets a wider range of retailers, including smaller and mid-market brands. Comenity issues over 100 different co-branded and private-label cards for brands like Ulta Beauty, Victoria's Secret, Wayfair, and many others. If you have ever been offered a store card at a mid-size retailer, there is a good chance Comenity is behind it.

**Goldman Sachs** entered the co-brand market with the **Apple Card** in 2019, representing a new kind of partnership: a technology company pairing with a Wall Street bank to create a digital-first credit card. The Apple Card had no physical application form, no paper statements, and lived primarily inside the Apple Wallet. It proved that the co-brand model could be reimagined for the smartphone era — though Goldman's consumer banking ambitions have since been scaled back, with the Apple Card portfolio reportedly transitioning away from Goldman.

## The Problem With Legacy Co-Brand Models

Despite the success of co-branded cards, the traditional approach has significant limitations.

**The <GlossaryTerm term="White-Label Card" definition="A card product built by one company but branded and distributed by another, with the underlying provider remaining invisible to the end consumer.">white-label</GlossaryTerm> approach rebuilds from scratch each time.** When a traditional co-brand issuer like Synchrony takes on a new partner, much of the work — integration, compliance setup, program configuration — has to be done again for each new brand. The underlying technology was not built to be modular. Each program is a custom project, not a configuration change.

**The customer experience is fragmented.** If you have a retailer's co-branded card, managing it often means logging into an entirely separate website — not the retailer's own site, but the bank's portal or a dedicated servicing site. The experience feels disconnected from the brand. The retailer has limited control over the card management interface, the application flow, or the servicing experience. The bank runs all of that.

**Timelines are long.** Launching a traditional co-brand program can take 12 to 24 months. Negotiations with the issuing bank, regulatory approvals, technology integration, compliance review, card design and production — the process is sequential and slow. For a fast-moving digital brand, that timeline is often a dealbreaker.

**The bar for entry is high.** Traditional co-brand issuers want partners with large, established customer bases. If you are a mid-market SaaS platform with 50,000 business users who might benefit from a credit card, you are too small for Synchrony or Comenity to build a custom program. The economics do not work at that scale under the legacy model.

## What CCaaS Changes

Credit Card as a Service flips the traditional model by making card issuance modular, API-driven, and accessible to a much wider range of brands.

Instead of negotiating a bespoke partnership with a bank, a brand connects to a CCaaS platform through APIs. The platform provides the banking relationship (via a sponsor bank), the processing infrastructure, the underwriting engine, the compliance framework, and the card management tools — all as a managed service.

The key shifts:

**API-driven integration.** Rather than a 12-month waterfall project, the brand integrates with the CCaaS provider's APIs. Card applications, account management, transaction data, rewards configuration — it is all accessible programmatically. A well-resourced engineering team can get a card program to pilot stage in weeks, not years.

**Modular and repeatable.** The CCaaS platform's infrastructure is built once and configured many times. Adding a new brand partner does not require rebuilding the compliance stack or reconnecting to the card network. The platform already has its MIP/VIP connections, its sponsor bank relationships, and its regulatory framework in place.

**Brand controls the experience.** In a CCaaS model, the credit card application, approval, and management can live entirely within the brand's own app or website. The consumer never has to leave the brand's ecosystem. The brand decides how the card is presented, how rewards are displayed, and how servicing works.

**Faster time-to-market.** Because the infrastructure is pre-built, a new card program can launch in months rather than years. Some CCaaS providers advertise launch timelines of 8 to 12 weeks for a basic program.

## CCaaS vs. BaaS: Why Credit Changes Everything

If you have read about Banking as a Service (BaaS) or modern issuer processors like Marqeta and Stripe Issuing, you might wonder: how is CCaaS different?

The distinction is critical. Most BaaS and modern card-issuing platforms focus on **debit cards** and **prepaid cards**. Marqeta, Stripe Issuing, and similar providers excel at helping companies issue cards that draw from a funded account — a corporate expense account, a gig-worker payout balance, or a digital wallet. These are powerful tools, but they do not involve consumer lending.

Credit cards are fundamentally different because they involve **extending credit to consumers**. That means:

**Capital requirements.** Someone has to fund the loans. When a consumer charges $5,000 on a credit card and pays it back over six months, that capital has to come from somewhere. A CCaaS provider must either have its own balance sheet, partner with a bank that provides capital, or arrange warehouse lending facilities.

**Underwriting.** The provider needs to decide who qualifies for credit, at what limit, and at what APR. This requires credit modeling, risk analytics, and the ability to manage a portfolio of consumer loans — skills that debit-focused platforms do not need.

**Regulatory complexity.** Consumer credit is heavily regulated. Truth in Lending Act (TILA) disclosures, Regulation Z compliance, fair lending requirements, credit reporting obligations, dispute resolution rules — the regulatory burden for credit products is substantially heavier than for debit or prepaid.

This is what makes CCaaS a distinct category. It is not just card-issuing infrastructure (which BaaS providers offer). It is the full stack of credit issuance: capital, underwriting, compliance, and card processing, bundled into an API-accessible platform.

## Key CCaaS Players and Approaches

The CCaaS landscape is still young, but several players have carved out positions.

**Deserve** has become one of the most prominent CCaaS platforms, originally gaining attention for its work with underserved credit segments. Deserve pioneered <GlossaryTerm term="Alternative Underwriting" definition="Credit assessment methods that go beyond traditional credit scores, using data like transaction history, platform activity, or cash flow patterns to evaluate borrower risk.">alternative underwriting</GlossaryTerm> approaches for populations that traditional credit scores miss — international students, recent immigrants, and young adults with thin credit files. Rather than relying solely on FICO scores, Deserve incorporated data like educational background, visa status, and banking activity to make credit decisions.

Deserve has since expanded beyond its student roots, offering its platform to brands that want to launch credit card programs with customized rewards and embedded user experiences. The company provides the underwriting models, the sponsor bank relationship, the compliance infrastructure, and the APIs — allowing brands to focus on the customer-facing experience.

**How CCaaS differs from pure processors:** Companies like FIS (which now owns TSYS) and Fiserv are processors — they handle the transaction plumbing. They connect to Visa and Mastercard, run authorization logic, manage clearing and settlement. But they do not provide underwriting, capital, or a sponsor bank. A brand that wants to launch a credit card using FIS as its processor still needs to separately find a bank partner, set up credit models, arrange funding, and handle compliance. CCaaS bundles all of that together.

**How CCaaS differs from BaaS providers:** Marqeta, Stripe Issuing, and similar BaaS-adjacent platforms have built extraordinary card-issuing infrastructure. But their sweet spot is debit, prepaid, and corporate cards. They provide the APIs for card issuance and transaction management, but they do not provide consumer credit underwriting or balance-sheet capital for revolving credit lines. A brand using Marqeta can issue a debit card in days. Issuing a credit card requires an entirely different set of capabilities that CCaaS providers are purpose-built to deliver.

## The Market Opportunity

The numbers reveal why CCaaS is attracting investment and attention.

There are approximately 1.12 billion credit cards in circulation in the United States. The average American holds four cards and carries roughly $6,500 in credit card debt. Total US credit card balances exceeded $1.1 trillion in recent years. Interest and fee revenue from credit cards generates tens of billions of dollars annually for issuing banks.

But this enormous market is concentrated among a small number of players. The six largest credit card issuers control the vast majority of outstanding balances. Community banks and credit unions — despite numbering in the thousands — collectively hold only a small fraction of the credit card market. And non-bank brands have had almost no way to participate directly.

CCaaS opens this market to a new set of entrants. Consider the possibilities:

A **vertical SaaS platform** serving dentists or contractors could offer its users a credit card that earns rewards on industry-specific spending categories — dental supplies, building materials — while also providing the platform with transaction data insights.

An **e-commerce marketplace** could offer sellers a credit card that provides working capital tied to their sales velocity, with credit limits that adjust automatically based on platform performance.

A **loyalty program** that currently offers only points and discounts could add a credit card that deepens engagement and creates a new revenue stream through interchange sharing.

None of these players would have been viable co-brand partners under the traditional Synchrony or Comenity model. They are too small, too niche, or too digital-first for the legacy approach. CCaaS makes them viable.

## Ethical Considerations: More Credit Is Not Always Better

Any honest discussion of making credit cards easier to launch must grapple with a difficult reality: credit card debt is one of the most significant sources of financial stress for American consumers.

Approximately 37% of Americans carry a credit card balance from month to month, paying interest rates that average over 20% APR. Total credit card debt in the US exceeds $1 trillion. Late fees, penalty APRs, and compounding interest can trap cardholders in cycles of debt that take years to escape.

Expanding access to credit card products — which is what CCaaS enables — carries real risks. More card programs means more opportunities for consumers to take on debt they cannot comfortably repay. If CCaaS simply makes it easier for brands to push credit products on consumers who do not need them, the net impact on financial health could be negative.

But there is a counter-argument. The concentration of credit card issuance among a few large banks has not exactly produced consumer-friendly outcomes. APRs remain high. Fee structures are complex. Rewards programs are increasingly devalued. A more competitive market — with more issuers offering differentiated products — could drive better terms for consumers.

The CCaaS model's emphasis on alternative underwriting also has the potential to be a force for financial inclusion. Traditional credit scoring excludes millions of creditworthy people — immigrants, young adults, gig workers — who lack the conventional credit history that FICO scores require. Alternative underwriting models that consider cash flow, employment, and platform activity data can extend credit responsibly to people who would otherwise be shut out.

The key word is "responsibly." The technology to launch credit cards faster and more broadly now exists. Whether that technology is deployed in ways that genuinely serve consumers — or primarily extract value from them — depends on the choices made by the platforms, the regulators, and the brands that use CCaaS to enter the credit market.

## Where This Is Heading

CCaaS is still in its early innings. The category is small relative to the overall credit card market, and the largest card programs are still built through traditional bank partnerships. But the trajectory is clear.

As more brands look to deepen customer relationships — and as interchange revenue sharing becomes a proven business model — the demand for accessible credit card infrastructure will grow. The brands that once would have settled for a simple co-branded debit card or a buy-now-pay-later integration will increasingly ask: can we offer our own credit card?

CCaaS providers are betting the answer is yes — and that the next generation of credit card products will not come from the six largest banks, but from the platforms, marketplaces, and brands that know their customers best.
