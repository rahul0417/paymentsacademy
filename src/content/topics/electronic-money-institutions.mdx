## The License Behind the Fintech Revolution

Every time you tap a Revolut card, send money through Wise, or top up a digital wallet in Europe, there is a good chance that the company handling your money is not a bank. It is an <GlossaryTerm term="Electronic Money (E-Money)" definition="A digital representation of fiat currency stored electronically that allows customers to load funds, make payments, and execute transfers. E-money is not a bank deposit — it is a stored monetary value represented by a claim on the issuer.">Electronic Money Institution</GlossaryTerm> — an EMI. These institutions are the regulatory backbone of digital payments and neobanking across Europe and beyond. They power the accounts, wallets, and cards that hundreds of millions of people use daily, yet most consumers have never heard of them.

Understanding EMIs is essential for anyone working in fintech. They sit at the intersection of regulation, product design, and financial infrastructure — and they explain why companies like Revolut could launch banking-like services years before obtaining an actual banking license.

## What Is an EMI?

An Electronic Money Institution is a legal entity authorized by a financial regulator to issue electronic money. That definition sounds simple, but the details matter enormously.

<GlossaryTerm term="Electronic Money (E-Money)" definition="A digital representation of fiat currency stored electronically that allows customers to load funds, make payments, and execute transfers. E-money is not a bank deposit — it is a stored monetary value represented by a claim on the issuer.">Electronic money</GlossaryTerm> — or e-money — is a digital representation of fiat currency stored electronically. When you load funds into a Revolut account or top up a PayPal balance, that money becomes e-money. It is denominated in a real currency (euros, pounds, dollars), it can be used to make payments, and it can be transferred to other people. But here is the critical distinction: **e-money is not a bank deposit.**

Think of it like a prepaid account that a regulated company manages on your behalf. When you hand over cash to buy a prepaid card at a shop, the shop gives you a card with stored value. You can spend that value anywhere the card is accepted. But the money on that card is not sitting in a savings account earning interest at a bank — it is a claim on the card issuer. If you want your money back, you redeem it from the issuer, not from a bank.

An EMI works on the same principle, but at a vastly larger scale and under strict regulatory oversight. The EMI receives your funds, issues an equivalent amount of e-money, and then <GlossaryTerm term="Safeguarding" definition="The regulatory requirement for EMIs to protect customer funds by holding 100% of e-money value in segregated accounts, insurance, or low-risk assets — ensuring funds are ring-fenced from the EMI's own operations and creditors.">safeguards</GlossaryTerm> those funds — keeping them separate from its own money — so that your balance is always backed one-to-one by real currency. You can spend it, send it, or convert it back to cash at any time.

This legal framework — established by the Electronic Money Directive (EMD1 in 2000, revised as EMD2 in 2009) — created an entirely new category of regulated financial institution. It was designed to foster innovation in payments by allowing non-bank companies to handle customer money under a lighter regulatory framework than full banking.

## What EMIs Can and Cannot Do

The EMI license is deliberately scoped. It grants meaningful capabilities while drawing firm boundaries around banking activities that require a full banking license.

**What EMIs can do:**

- **Issue electronic money.** This includes digital wallets, prepaid cards, stored-value accounts, and any other mechanism for holding customer funds electronically. This is the core function of an EMI.
- **Provide payment services.** EMIs can facilitate transfers between accounts, process remittances, and execute payment transactions. Most EMIs in Europe are also authorized as payment service providers under PSD2.
- **Hold customer funds as e-money.** Customers can load money into their EMI-issued accounts and maintain balances, functionally equivalent to a current account for everyday spending.
- **Issue payment instruments.** EMIs can issue debit cards (typically Visa or Mastercard), virtual cards, and virtual IBANs that allow customers to receive bank transfers.
- **Facilitate currency exchange.** Many EMIs offer multi-currency accounts and foreign exchange services, converting between currencies at competitive rates.

**What EMIs cannot do:**

- **Accept deposits in the legal banking sense.** E-money balances are not deposits. This distinction has real consequences — e-money is not protected by deposit insurance schemes.
- **Lend money or extend credit.** EMIs cannot offer loans, overdrafts, or credit lines. This is the single biggest limitation and the primary reason many fintechs eventually pursue a full banking license.
- **Pay interest on e-money balances.** EMD2 explicitly prohibits this. Your Revolut account (when operating under an EMI license) cannot pay you interest on your balance. This prevents EMIs from competing directly with bank savings accounts.
- **Provide investment services.** EMIs cannot offer brokerage, asset management, or securities trading under their EMI authorization (though they may obtain separate licenses for this).
- **Use customer funds for their own operations.** Safeguarding rules strictly prohibit EMIs from mixing customer money with corporate funds or using customer money for lending, investment, or operational expenses.

These boundaries create a clear trade-off. An EMI can offer most of the day-to-day financial services that consumers need — spending, sending, receiving, holding money — but it cannot generate revenue from lending (the primary profit engine of banking) or attract savers with interest rates.

## EMI vs Bank vs Payment Institution

The payments landscape has three primary categories of licensed entity, and understanding their differences is critical for evaluating any fintech's regulatory position.

**Banks** hold full banking licenses (or charters, in the US). They can accept deposits protected by deposit insurance (up to €100,000 in the EU, £85,000 in the UK, $250,000 under US FDIC), lend money, pay interest, and offer the complete range of financial services. The trade-off is cost and complexity: minimum capital requirements start at €5 million or more in the EU, licensing takes 12-36 months, and the ongoing compliance burden is substantial. Banks are the most heavily regulated financial institutions.

**EMIs** occupy the middle ground. They can issue e-money and provide payment services but cannot lend or accept deposits. Customer funds are protected through <GlossaryTerm term="Safeguarding" definition="The regulatory requirement for EMIs to protect customer funds by holding 100% of e-money value in segregated accounts, insurance, or low-risk assets — ensuring funds are ring-fenced from the EMI's own operations and creditors.">safeguarding</GlossaryTerm> rather than deposit insurance — an important distinction we will explore in detail. Minimum capital is €350,000, licensing typically takes 6-15 months, and the regulatory burden is lighter than banking but still significant.

**Payment Institutions (PIs)** are the lightest category. Authorized under PSD2, they can provide payment services — transfers, remittances, payment processing — but cannot issue e-money or hold customer funds beyond what is needed to execute a specific transaction. Capital requirements range from €20,000 to €125,000 depending on the services offered. PIs are common among payment processors and money transfer operators.

The key tension is between fund protection methods. When you deposit money at a bank, it is protected by a government-backed deposit insurance scheme. If the bank fails, the insurance scheme pays you back up to the insured limit. When you hold e-money at an EMI, there is no deposit insurance. Instead, the EMI is required to safeguard your funds — segregating them from its own money so they can be returned to you even if the EMI goes insolvent. Safeguarding provides strong protection, but it is not the same as a government guarantee.

This distinction matters most in extreme scenarios. If a bank collapses, deposit insurance pays out quickly and reliably — it is backed by the state. If an EMI collapses, your safeguarded funds should be ring-fenced and returned to you, but the process may take longer and the outcome depends on how well the EMI implemented its safeguarding obligations. In practice, well-run EMIs with proper safeguarding arrangements provide strong customer protection. But the theoretical difference in backstop — state guarantee versus regulatory requirement — is real.

## EMI License Types

Not all EMIs are created equal. Regulators offer two tiers of authorization, designed to match regulatory burden to business scale.

### Full EMI License

The full EMI authorization is required for any firm that expects to handle significant volumes of e-money. Key characteristics:

- **Minimum capital:** €350,000 (or 2% of average outstanding e-money, whichever is higher)
- **No volume limits:** The firm can issue unlimited e-money and process unlimited payment transactions
- **EEA passporting:** A full EMI licensed in any EU/EEA member state can passport its services across all 30 EEA countries without obtaining separate licenses in each jurisdiction — a massive advantage for pan-European expansion
- **Full regulatory obligations:** Complete safeguarding requirements, governance standards, annual audits, and ongoing reporting

### Small EMI

The <GlossaryTerm term="Small EMI" definition="A lighter-weight EMI registration for firms with less than €5 million in average outstanding e-money and under €3 million in monthly payment transactions. Lower capital requirements but cannot passport services across borders.">Small EMI</GlossaryTerm> registration is a lighter-weight option for firms operating at smaller scale:

- **Minimum capital:** Starts at €50,000 (when outstanding e-money is under €1 million)
- **Volume caps:** Average outstanding e-money must stay below €5 million, and monthly payment transactions must remain under €3 million
- **No passporting:** A Small EMI can only operate in the member state where it is registered — it cannot use the EU passporting framework to offer services cross-border
- **Simpler governance:** Reduced reporting and governance requirements compared to a full EMI

The Small EMI route is ideal for startups testing product-market fit or operating in a single domestic market. But the moment a company needs to expand internationally within Europe or exceeds the volume caps, it must upgrade to a full EMI authorization.

In the UK, the equivalents are the **Authorized Electronic Money Institution (AEMI)** for the full license and the **Small Electronic Money Institution (SEMI)** for the lighter registration. Post-Brexit, UK EMI licenses no longer provide EU passporting rights, so firms needing to serve both UK and EU customers must obtain authorizations in both jurisdictions.

## The Licensing Process

Obtaining an EMI license is a significant undertaking. It requires substantial capital, extensive documentation, and months of engagement with the regulator.

### Timeline

End-to-end, the process typically takes **6-15 months**, depending on the jurisdiction and the quality of the application. Lithuania has become a popular jurisdiction for EMI licensing, partly because the Bank of Lithuania has streamlined the process to 6-9 months and offers a "newcomer program" to help applicants navigate requirements. Other jurisdictions — particularly larger ones like Germany (BaFin) or France (ACPR) — tend to take longer, often 12-15 months.

### Capital and Cost

The minimum regulatory capital for a full EMI is €350,000, but this is just the starting point. When you factor in legal fees, compliance consulting, technology infrastructure, and the cost of building the required team, the total investment to obtain and launch with an EMI license typically exceeds **€600,000 and can reach €1 million or more**. Roughly:

- €350,000+ in regulatory capital (must be maintained, not spent)
- €100,000-200,000 in legal and consulting fees
- €100,000-200,000 in technology and infrastructure setup
- €50,000-100,000 in initial staffing (MLRO, compliance, operations)

### Required Infrastructure

Regulators will not grant an EMI license to a company that merely has capital. They require evidence of a robust operational framework:

- **Money Laundering Reporting Officer (MLRO):** A named, qualified individual responsible for AML compliance
- **AML/CFT program:** Documented policies and procedures for anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing
- **KYC and Customer Due Diligence (CDD) procedures:** How the firm will verify customer identity, assess risk, and monitor ongoing relationships
- **Risk management framework:** Enterprise-wide risk identification, assessment, and mitigation
- **IT security and data protection:** Demonstrated cybersecurity measures, penetration testing, and GDPR compliance
- **Transaction monitoring:** Systems to detect and report suspicious activity
- **Safeguarding arrangements:** Pre-agreed arrangements with a credit institution for segregated accounts
- **PCI DSS compliance:** Required if the EMI will handle card data

### Key Documents

The application itself requires a comprehensive package:

- **Three-year business plan** with financial projections, market analysis, and growth strategy
- **Governance framework** detailing organizational structure, internal controls, and reporting lines
- **Proof of capital** showing the required funds are available and sourced legitimately
- **Fit-and-proper assessments** for all directors, senior managers, and qualifying shareholders — demonstrating competence, integrity, and financial soundness

The rigor of this process is deliberate. Regulators are granting the applicant the right to hold and move other people's money. Every element of the application is designed to ensure the firm can do so responsibly.

## Safeguarding: How EMIs Protect Your Money

This is the most important concept in EMI regulation. Because e-money is not covered by deposit insurance, the entire framework for protecting customer funds rests on safeguarding. Get this wrong, and customers lose their money. Get it right, and it provides protection that, in practice, is comparable to deposit insurance for everyday use.

EMD2 permits three methods of safeguarding, and an EMI must implement at least one:

### Method 1: Segregation (Most Common)

The most widely used approach. The EMI deposits customer funds into a **segregated account** held at a separate credit institution (a bank). These funds must be deposited by the end of the business day following the day they are received (T+1). The segregated account is legally ring-fenced — if the EMI becomes insolvent, the funds in the segregated account do not form part of the EMI's estate and cannot be claimed by the EMI's creditors.

This is the method most EMIs use because it is straightforward, well-understood by auditors and regulators, and provides clear legal protection.

### Method 2: Insurance or Guarantee

The EMI obtains an **insurance policy** or **bank guarantee** that covers the full value of outstanding e-money. If the EMI fails, the insurer or guarantor pays out the protected amount. This method is less common because obtaining insurance or guarantees at the required scale can be expensive and difficult to arrange.

### Method 3: Investment in Low-Risk Assets

The EMI invests customer funds in **low-risk, liquid assets** — typically government bonds or other approved instruments. The assets must be sufficiently liquid to be converted to cash quickly if needed. This method is the least common for operational e-money because of the additional complexity of managing an investment portfolio while ensuring immediate liquidity.

### Safeguarding Requirements

Regardless of which method an EMI uses, several universal requirements apply:

- **100% backing (1:1):** Every euro of outstanding e-money must be matched by an equal amount of safeguarded funds. There is no fractional reserve — unlike banks, which can lend out a portion of deposits.
- **Daily reconciliation:** The EMI must reconcile its outstanding e-money against its safeguarded funds every business day, identifying and resolving any discrepancies immediately.
- **Annual safeguarding audit:** An independent auditor must verify the EMI's safeguarding arrangements annually and report findings to the regulator.
- **Organizational controls:** The EMI must have documented procedures, named responsible individuals, and internal controls governing the safeguarding process.

The safeguarding framework means that, in a well-run EMI, customer money is actually very well protected. This is why the UK FCA has been tightening safeguarding rules, with significant reforms taking effect from May 2026.

## Notable EMIs

Some of the most prominent names in fintech operate — or started their journeys — as EMIs. Their stories illustrate how the EMI license functions as both a launchpad and a strategic choice.

**Revolut** obtained its EMI license from the Bank of Lithuania in 2018, which allowed it to passport services across Europe. For years, Revolut operated as an EMI — offering accounts, cards, currency exchange, and crypto trading to over 40 million customers — without being a bank. The company applied for a UK banking license and waited through a multi-year process before finally receiving approval in September 2024. Valued at approximately $75 billion, Revolut demonstrates both the power of the EMI model (you can build a massive fintech without a banking license) and its limitations (the banking license was needed to unlock lending and deposit-taking).

**Wise (formerly TransferWise)** is perhaps the most deliberate example of an EMI that has chosen to remain an EMI. Authorized by the UK FCA, Wise has built a multi-billion-dollar business around international money transfers and multi-currency accounts without pursuing a banking license. The company's view is that a banking license would bring complexity and regulatory burden that does not align with its core product. Wise proves that the EMI model can be a permanent strategic choice, not just a stepping stone.

**Monzo** followed the classic neobank trajectory. It launched as an EMI in 2016 with a prepaid card, used the EMI period to build a customer base and demonstrate operational capability, and then obtained a full UK banking license in 2017 — a remarkably fast transition. In 2025, Monzo secured an EU banking license in Ireland to serve European customers post-Brexit.

**Stripe** holds e-money licenses from the UK FCA and the Central Bank of Ireland, enabling it to provide payment services and hold funds for its platform customers across Europe. For Stripe, the EMI license is infrastructure — it supports the company's payment processing and treasury products rather than consumer-facing accounts.

**PayPal** operates as an EMI in Europe through an authorization from the Central Bank of Luxembourg. When European customers hold a PayPal balance, that balance is e-money issued under PayPal's Luxembourg EMI license, with funds safeguarded according to EU requirements.

## The EMI-to-Bank Pipeline

For many fintechs — particularly <GlossaryTerm term="Neobank" definition="A fully digital-only bank that operates without physical branches, leveraging modern technology and APIs to deliver banking services through mobile apps and web interfaces.">neobanks</GlossaryTerm> — the EMI license is the first stop on a deliberate journey toward full banking.

### Why Start as an EMI?

The economics and timelines are compelling. An EMI license requires €350,000 in capital and takes 6-12 months. A banking license requires €5 million or more and takes 12-36 months. For a startup trying to get to market, validate its product, and begin generating revenue, the EMI route is dramatically faster and cheaper.

More importantly, an EMI can offer most of the services that consumers actually want from a "bank": an account to hold money, a card to spend it, the ability to send and receive transfers, and currency exchange. For the majority of everyday financial interactions, an EMI-powered account is functionally indistinguishable from a bank account. Most consumers neither know nor care about the legal distinction.

### The Ceiling

The EMI license has a hard ceiling, and eventually most ambitious fintechs hit it.

**Lending** is the primary revenue engine of banking. Net interest income — the spread between what a bank pays depositors and what it earns from borrowers — accounts for the majority of revenue at most banks. An EMI cannot lend, which means it must generate all its revenue from payments-related activities: interchange, foreign exchange margins, subscription fees, and platform fees. These are viable revenue streams, but they are structurally thinner than lending.

**Interest on balances** is prohibited for EMIs under EMD2. In a high-interest-rate environment, this becomes a significant competitive disadvantage. Banks can offer 4-5% on savings accounts. EMIs cannot offer anything. Customers with meaningful savings will park them at a bank rather than leave them at an EMI earning zero.

**Deposit insurance** is a trust factor. Consumers and businesses feel more comfortable knowing their funds are protected by a government-backed scheme. Safeguarding provides real protection, but it lacks the simplicity and brand recognition of "FDIC insured" or "FSCS protected."

### The Transition

The journey from EMI to bank varies dramatically. Revolut spent years pursuing its UK banking license, navigating a lengthy regulatory process that included multiple delays and extensive scrutiny of its compliance infrastructure. Monzo was faster — moving from EMI to bank in roughly a year — though it had the advantage of building its banking application in parallel with its EMI operations.

Some neobanks skip the EMI phase entirely. N26 in Germany and Starling Bank in the UK went directly for banking licenses from the start, accepting the longer timeline and higher capital requirements in exchange for immediate access to lending and deposit-taking.

The choice between starting as an EMI and going straight for a banking license is strategic. It depends on the company's capital, its product roadmap, its target market, and its founders' appetite for regulatory complexity.

## Global Variations

The EMI concept originated in Europe, but similar frameworks exist worldwide — with significant differences in structure, scope, and requirements.

**European Union:** EMD2 is the governing framework. Full EMI authorization requires €350,000 in capital and grants passporting rights across all 30 EEA countries. The EU framework is the most mature and widely replicated EMI regime globally.

**United Kingdom:** Post-Brexit, the UK operates its own EMI regime under the FCA, separate from the EU. UK-authorized EMIs can no longer passport into the EU and vice versa. The FCA has been actively strengthening its oversight of EMIs, particularly around safeguarding practices, with new rules expected from May 2026.

**Singapore:** The Payment Services Act 2019 created a unified licensing framework with two tiers: the Standard Payment Institution (SPI) license for smaller operators and the Major Payment Institution (MPI) license for firms exceeding specified thresholds. The MPI license covers e-money issuance alongside other payment services under a single authorization.

**India:** The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) regulates e-money-like products through the Prepaid Payment Instrument (PPI) license. PPIs include mobile wallets (like Paytm Wallet) and prepaid cards. The minimum capital requirement is 5 crore rupees (approximately €550,000). India's framework is distinct in that it operates alongside the massively successful UPI real-time payment system, which has reduced the importance of stored-value wallets.

**United States:** There is no federal equivalent to the EMI license. Companies that want to hold and transmit customer funds must obtain **Money Transmitter Licenses (MTLs)** on a state-by-state basis — a notoriously painful process covering all 50 states plus territories. The full process typically takes 12-24 months and costs over $1 million in legal fees, surety bonds, and application costs. This fragmented approach is one reason why US fintech companies often rely on bank partnerships rather than obtaining their own licenses.

**Other notable regimes:** Hong Kong has the Stored Value Facility (SVF) license. Brazil has the Payment Institution (Instituicao de Pagamento) authorization. Switzerland introduced a dedicated Fintech license in 2019 allowing firms to accept public deposits up to CHF 100 million without a full banking license. Each reflects local regulatory philosophy and market structure, but all address the same fundamental question: how do you regulate non-bank entities that hold and move people's money?

## The Future: PSD3 and the Unified License

The European regulatory landscape for EMIs is about to undergo its most significant transformation since EMD2 was introduced in 2009.

In November 2025, the EU reached political agreement on the **PSD3/PSR framework** — a comprehensive overhaul of European payment services regulation. The most consequential change for EMIs: **EMD2 will be repealed and absorbed into PSD3.** The separate legal category of "Electronic Money Institution" will cease to exist as a standalone authorization. Instead, EMIs will become a sub-category of payment institutions under a single, unified authorization framework.

What this means in practice:

- **Single unified license.** Instead of separate EMI and PI authorizations, there will be one payment institution license that covers both e-money issuance and payment services. This simplifies the regulatory landscape and eliminates the sometimes-confusing overlap between EMI and PI activities.
- **Existing EMIs must re-apply.** Firms currently authorized as EMIs will need to transition to the new framework, submitting updated applications under the revised requirements. This is not automatic — regulators will use the transition as an opportunity to reassess compliance and governance.
- **Expected timeline: approximately 2028.** The PSD3/PSR framework is expected to be formally adopted with an 18-24 month transition period, putting the effective date around 2028. EMIs should be planning for this transition now.

Beyond PSD3, several other regulatory developments are reshaping the EMI landscape:

**UK FCA safeguarding reform.** The FCA has been increasingly concerned about safeguarding practices at UK EMIs and has introduced new, more prescriptive rules taking effect from May 2026. These rules will require EMIs to hold safeguarded funds in a statutory trust (rather than relying solely on segregation), improve client money record-keeping, and submit more frequent regulatory returns. This represents a significant tightening of the UK safeguarding regime.

**The digital euro.** The European Central Bank is developing a digital euro — a central bank digital currency (CBDC) for retail use. If launched, the digital euro could complement or compete with e-money issued by EMIs. The exact relationship between CBDC wallets and EMI-issued e-money is still being defined, but EMIs are paying close attention. A widely adopted digital euro could reduce demand for EMI-issued e-money, or it could create new opportunities for EMIs to distribute digital euros alongside their existing services.

The broader trend is clear: regulators are raising the bar for non-bank financial institutions while simultaneously simplifying the licensing framework. The era of obtaining a lightweight EMI license and operating with minimal oversight is ending. The EMIs that thrive in this new environment will be those that have invested in robust compliance infrastructure, strong governance, and genuine safeguarding discipline — not just the minimum required to obtain a license, but the operational excellence required to maintain one.

## Why EMIs Matter

EMIs occupy a unique and essential position in the financial ecosystem. They created the regulatory space for an entire generation of fintech innovation — enabling companies to offer banking-like services without the full cost and complexity of a banking license. Without the EMI framework, Revolut, Wise, PayPal, and hundreds of other fintechs would have needed to become banks or remain dependent on bank partnerships indefinitely.

At the same time, EMIs illustrate a fundamental truth about financial regulation: every regulatory shortcut comes with a trade-off. The EMI license is faster and cheaper than a banking license, but it restricts lending, prohibits interest, and replaces deposit insurance with safeguarding. These trade-offs are manageable for payments-focused businesses but limiting for companies that aspire to offer the full range of banking services.

For anyone building, investing in, or working alongside fintech companies, understanding EMIs is not optional. They are the regulatory foundation that most European fintechs are built on. Knowing what an EMI can do, what it cannot do, and how it protects customer money will help you evaluate any fintech's capabilities, limitations, and strategic trajectory.
