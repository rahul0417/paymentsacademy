## The Bank Inside the Software

Imagine you run a small hair salon. You use scheduling software to book appointments, manage staff, and track revenue. One day, that same software offers you a business checking account — right inside the dashboard you already use. No visiting a bank branch. No paperwork. A few taps and you have an account with a debit card, and your daily sales revenue is deposited automatically every evening. Need a short-term loan to buy new equipment? The software already knows your revenue history, so it offers you $15,000 at a rate calculated from your actual transaction data. You accept with one click.

That's <GlossaryTerm term="Embedded Finance" definition="The integration of financial services — such as payments, lending, insurance, or accounts — directly into non-financial software platforms and applications.">embedded finance</GlossaryTerm>. Financial services — accounts, cards, lending, insurance — delivered not by a bank, but through the software you already use to run your business. The bank is still there, behind the scenes, providing the regulated infrastructure. But to the salon owner, the experience is seamless: the software is the bank.

This isn't a hypothetical. Platforms like Shopify, Toast, Mindbody, and hundreds of vertical SaaS companies now offer financial products alongside their core software. And the economic incentive is powerful: a software platform that adds financial services can increase its revenue per customer by 2-5x. The financial products often generate more revenue than the software subscription itself.

## The SaaS Evolution: From Software to Financial Platform

To understand why embedded finance is happening now, it helps to see the progression of how software platforms have evolved their relationship with money.

**SaaS 1.0: Software only.** The platform sells software subscriptions. A restaurant management system charges $99/month for table management, scheduling, and inventory. Revenue comes exclusively from subscription fees. The platform has no involvement in the money flowing through the business. When a diner pays the restaurant, that payment goes through a separate payment processor the restaurant set up independently. The software company and the money are completely separate.

**SaaS 2.0: Software plus payments.** The platform integrates payment processing. Now the restaurant management system also processes card payments at the table. The platform earns its $99/month subscription plus a percentage of every transaction — typically 2.5-2.9% plus a per-transaction fee. Revenue per customer jumps significantly because the platform now participates in the restaurant's gross payment volume, not just a fixed monthly fee. Stripe, Square, and Toast all enabled this shift. For a restaurant processing $50,000/month in card payments, the platform earns roughly $1,250/month from payment processing alone — more than 10x the subscription fee.

**SaaS 3.0: Software plus financial services.** The platform goes further, offering deposit accounts, business cards, lending, and insurance. The restaurant management system now holds the restaurant's operating funds, issues a branded debit card for purchasing supplies, offers a working capital loan based on daily sales data, and bundles equipment insurance. Revenue per customer multiplies again because the platform captures interchange from card spending, earns interest on deposits, and takes a share of loan origination revenue. The platform has evolved from a software tool into a financial operating system.

Each stage represents a deeper economic relationship with the customer. SaaS 1.0 captures $100/month. SaaS 2.0 captures $1,000/month. SaaS 3.0 can capture $2,000-5,000/month from the same customer. That's the economic gravity pulling every vertical SaaS platform toward embedded finance.

## What Is Banking-as-a-Service?

Embedded finance is what the end customer sees. <GlossaryTerm term="Banking-as-a-Service (BaaS)" definition="A model where licensed banks expose their infrastructure through APIs, allowing non-bank companies to offer financial products like accounts, cards, and loans without obtaining their own banking license.">Banking-as-a-Service</GlossaryTerm> is the infrastructure layer that makes it possible.

Here's the fundamental problem: offering financial products requires a banking license. Opening deposit accounts, issuing cards linked to card networks, originating loans — all of these activities are regulated and require a chartered bank with the appropriate licenses, capital requirements, and compliance programs. Obtaining a bank charter takes years and costs tens of millions of dollars. No software company wants to become a bank just to offer their customers a business account.

BaaS solves this by letting licensed banks expose their banking infrastructure through APIs. A software platform connects to the BaaS layer, and the BaaS provider connects to a <GlossaryTerm term="Sponsor Bank" definition="A chartered, regulated bank that partners with fintech companies or platforms to provide the banking license, compliance framework, and settlement capabilities needed to offer financial products.">sponsor bank</GlossaryTerm> that provides the actual banking license, regulatory compliance, and access to payment networks. The platform gets to offer banking products under its own brand. The sponsor bank provides the regulated foundation. The BaaS provider stitches the two together with technology.

Think of it like cloud computing for financial services. Just as AWS lets any company build a web application without owning data centers, BaaS lets any software platform offer financial products without owning a bank.

**Layer 1: The sponsor bank.** At the foundation is a chartered, regulated bank. This bank holds the licenses — state banking charter, FDIC membership, Federal Reserve access. It provides the legal authority to hold deposits, issue cards, and originate loans. It also provides access to payment rails: ACH for bank transfers, Visa/Mastercard for card transactions, Fedwire for wire transfers. The sponsor bank is ultimately responsible for regulatory compliance — even when the end customer never interacts with the bank directly.

**Layer 2: The BaaS middleware platform.** Companies like Unit, Treasury Prime, Bond, Synapse (before its collapse — more on this later), and Stripe Treasury sit in the middle. They build the API layer that translates complex banking operations into clean developer interfaces. Opening an account becomes a POST request. Issuing a card becomes another API call. The middleware handles the integration with the sponsor bank's <GlossaryTerm term="Core Banking System" definition="The back-end software that processes daily banking transactions — account management, ledgering, interest calculations, and regulatory reporting.">core banking system</GlossaryTerm>, manages KYC/KYB workflows, handles transaction monitoring, and provides dashboards for the platform to manage its program.

**Layer 3: The platform or brand.** This is the software company that the end customer actually interacts with. The platform builds the user experience — the account opening flow, the card management interface, the lending application. It markets the financial products to its existing user base. The platform's brand is what the customer sees. The sponsor bank's name might appear in fine print ("Banking services provided by Evolve Bank & Trust, Member FDIC"), but the experience is entirely the platform's.

## Key Products

BaaS infrastructure enables three core financial products, each with distinct mechanics and economics.

### Deposit Accounts

The platform offers business or consumer checking accounts. These are real bank accounts, held at the sponsor bank, with pass-through FDIC insurance up to $250,000 per depositor. The accounts can receive ACH deposits, send payments, and hold balances. The platform typically earns revenue from the interest spread — the sponsor bank pays the platform a portion of the interest earned on deposits — and from interchange on associated debit cards.

For the end customer, the value proposition is compelling: an account integrated directly into the software they already use, with no separate bank login, no branch visits, and automatic reconciliation between their business transactions and their account balance.

### Card Issuing

The platform issues branded debit or prepaid cards — virtual, physical, or both — linked to the customer's account. Every time the customer uses the card, the platform earns a share of the interchange fee (typically 1-1.8% for debit). For a business spending $20,000/month on a platform-branded debit card, that's $200-360/month in interchange revenue to the platform.

Virtual cards can be issued instantly via API. Physical cards require manufacturing and shipping but create stronger brand presence. Many platforms start with virtual cards for immediate value, then follow up with physical cards for ongoing engagement.

The card issuing infrastructure connects through an issuer processor (Marqeta, Lithic, Highnote) to the card networks (Visa, Mastercard). The sponsor bank provides the BIN range — the block of card numbers — and the settlement capabilities.

### Lending

This is where platform data becomes uniquely powerful. A traditional bank evaluating a small business loan has limited information — credit scores, tax returns, maybe a few months of bank statements. A software platform that processes the business's daily transactions has real-time revenue data, seasonal patterns, customer retention metrics, and operational performance indicators.

A restaurant management platform knows the restaurant's daily covers, average ticket size, tip percentages, cancellation rates, and year-over-year growth. That's vastly more data than a bank loan officer gets from a two-page application. Platforms can use this data to underwrite loans faster, more accurately, and for businesses that traditional banks would reject.

The lending products typically take two forms. Revenue-based advances (like Shopify Capital or Square Loans) offer a lump sum repaid as a fixed percentage of daily sales — no fixed monthly payments, so the business pays more when sales are strong and less when they're slow. Term loans offer a fixed amount with a fixed repayment schedule, underwritten using platform data but structured like a traditional loan.

Platforms typically partner with a lending-licensed entity (often the same sponsor bank) and earn origination fees or a share of interest income. The loan capital may come from the sponsor bank's balance sheet, from institutional investors, or from credit facilities the BaaS provider arranges.

## Build vs. Buy: Two Paths to Embedded Finance

A software platform that wants to offer financial products faces a fundamental choice.

**Option 1: Use a BaaS platform.** Connect to a BaaS provider like Unit, Treasury Prime, or Stripe Treasury. The BaaS platform provides the APIs, the sponsor bank relationship, compliance tooling, and pre-built components for account opening, card issuance, and lending. Time to launch: weeks to a few months. Cost: transaction-based pricing, typically requiring no large upfront investment. Compliance team needed: minimal — the BaaS platform and sponsor bank handle most regulatory requirements.

**Option 2: Direct bank partnership.** Negotiate directly with a bank to build a custom program. The platform works directly with the bank's core banking system, builds its own compliance infrastructure, and manages the regulatory relationship. Time to launch: 18 months to 2+ years. Cost: $2 million or more in upfront investment for legal, compliance, and technology integration. Compliance team needed: 10-50 people, depending on product complexity, including BSA/AML officers, compliance attorneys, and audit staff.

The BaaS path is faster, cheaper, and requires less specialized expertise. It's the right choice for most platforms entering embedded finance for the first time. The direct bank partnership path offers more control, better unit economics at scale, and less dependency on a third-party provider. It makes sense for very large platforms processing billions in volume where the economics of the BaaS middleware layer become significant.

Some companies start with a BaaS provider to get to market quickly, then gradually bring more infrastructure in-house as they scale. Others eventually acquire or build their own bank — SoFi acquired Golden Pacific Bancorp to become a licensed bank, and LendingClub acquired Radius Bank for the same reason.

## Real-World Examples

**Shopify Balance.** Shopify offers its merchants a business account directly within the Shopify admin dashboard. Merchants can receive their Shopify sales revenue into their Balance account (rather than waiting for payouts to an external bank), earn cashback on a Shopify-branded card, and pay bills — all without leaving Shopify. For Shopify, this deepens merchant lock-in and generates interchange and deposit revenue. For merchants, it eliminates the friction of managing a separate bank account and reconciling transfers.

**Stripe Treasury and Issuing.** Stripe has built a full BaaS stack that its platform customers can use to offer financial products. Stripe Treasury provides the APIs to create FDIC-insured accounts. Stripe Issuing provides virtual and physical card creation. Stripe Capital provides lending based on Stripe processing data. Platforms like Shopify use Stripe's infrastructure under the hood. Stripe's approach is notable because it positions itself as the BaaS middleware layer, enabling other platforms to become embedded finance providers.

**Toast Capital.** Toast, the restaurant management platform, offers working capital loans to its restaurant customers. Because Toast processes the restaurant's card payments and has real-time visibility into daily sales, it can underwrite loans that traditional banks won't — a restaurant with strong daily revenue but a thin credit file can qualify based on Toast's data. Repayment is automatic, deducted as a percentage of daily card sales processed through Toast.

## Why It Matters

The promise of embedded finance goes beyond revenue for platforms. It addresses real gaps in how businesses and consumers access financial services.

Consider small businesses. According to Federal Reserve research, 55% of small businesses must visit bank branches for routine services — a significant burden for owners who work 50-60 hour weeks and can't afford to leave during business hours. Only 48% of small businesses report having adequate access to financing. Traditional banks often reject small business loan applications because they lack the granular data needed to underwrite a $25,000 loan profitably. The unit economics of traditional small business lending don't work for most banks at that loan size.

Embedded finance changes this equation. When financial services are delivered through the software a business already uses, the friction of accessing those services drops dramatically. No branch visits. No lengthy applications. No waiting weeks for a credit decision. And because the platform has rich operational data, it can underwrite customers that traditional banks can't assess.

For consumers, embedded finance appears as buy-now-pay-later at checkout, insurance offers within a travel booking app, or investment features inside a payroll platform. The common thread is context: financial products delivered at the moment they're relevant, through the interface the customer is already using.

## Risks and Hard Lessons

Embedded finance is not without significant risks. The industry has learned some painful lessons in recent years, and the regulatory environment is tightening.

**Regulatory scrutiny is increasing.** Bank regulators — the OCC, FDIC, and Federal Reserve — have grown concerned about the "rent-a-charter" model where sponsor banks lend their licenses to dozens of fintech partners without adequate oversight. In 2023 and 2024, multiple sponsor banks received consent orders from regulators citing deficiencies in their oversight of fintech partnerships. The message is clear: the sponsor bank is responsible for compliance, and regulators expect sponsor banks to actively supervise their fintech programs, not just provide a charter and collect fees.

**The Synapse collapse.** The most sobering cautionary tale in BaaS is the 2024 failure of Synapse, a prominent BaaS middleware provider. Synapse sat between fintech platforms and sponsor banks, managing ledgers and accounts for millions of end users. When Synapse filed for bankruptcy, a devastating problem emerged: the ledger records that tracked which end users owned which funds at the sponsor bank were unreliable. Tens of thousands of consumers and small businesses couldn't access their money for months. Some funds couldn't be reconciled at all.

The Synapse collapse revealed a critical architectural risk in the BaaS model. When the middleware layer fails, and the middleware is the system of record for account ownership, customers can lose access to their funds — even though the money still sits at an FDIC-insured bank. FDIC insurance protects against bank failure, not middleware failure.

**Lessons for platforms.** The Synapse episode underscores several principles for any company entering embedded finance:

*Avoid vendor lock-in.* Build modular architecture that allows switching BaaS providers or sponsor banks without losing customer data or account records. If your entire financial product depends on a single middleware vendor's proprietary ledger, you have a single point of failure.

*Understand the regulatory chain.* The sponsor bank is the regulated entity, but regulators increasingly expect platforms to maintain their own compliance programs. "We relied on our BaaS provider" is not an acceptable answer when something goes wrong.

*Custody and reconciliation matter.* Know where customer funds are held, how they're tracked, and how to reconcile balances independently of your middleware provider. If your provider disappeared tomorrow, could you account for every dollar?

*Due diligence on your partners.* Evaluate the financial health, regulatory standing, and operational resilience of both your BaaS provider and sponsor bank. A consent order against your sponsor bank can shut down your program with little notice.

## Where This Is Heading

Embedded finance is still in its early stages. Most estimates suggest that embedded financial services represent less than 5% of total financial services revenue today, but the share is growing rapidly as more software platforms add financial products.

The trend is toward deeper integration. Early embedded finance was limited to payments. Then came accounts and cards. Now platforms are adding lending, insurance, and even investment products. The long-term trajectory is the full financial operating system — a single platform where a business manages its operations, processes payments, holds funds, pays vendors, manages payroll, borrows capital, and insures its assets.

The regulatory framework is also maturing. Expect clearer guidelines on sponsor bank oversight, stronger requirements for middleware providers, and more direct regulatory scrutiny of the platforms themselves. The companies that build embedded finance on a solid compliance foundation — rather than treating compliance as someone else's problem — will be the ones that endure.

For payments professionals, the takeaway is straightforward: the line between software companies and financial services companies is disappearing. Understanding how BaaS infrastructure works, how sponsor bank relationships are structured, and how regulatory responsibility flows through the stack isn't just useful — it's becoming essential knowledge for anyone working in payments or fintech.
