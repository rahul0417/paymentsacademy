## The Invisible Engine Behind Every Card

When you tap your Chime debit card at a grocery store, the screen says "Approved" in about two seconds. You probably assume Chime's bank made that decision. In reality, a company you've likely never heard of — Galileo — received the authorization request, checked your balance, ran fraud rules, and sent back the approval. The bank was barely involved.

That company is an <GlossaryTerm term="Issuer Processor" definition="A technology company that handles transaction authorization, card management, and settlement on behalf of the issuing bank." />. It's the technology layer that sits between the issuing bank and the card network, doing the actual work of running a card program. If the issuing bank is the legal entity on the card, the issuer processor is the brain.

The US issuer processor market alone is worth roughly $8 billion as of 2025, projected to reach $15 billion by 2034. And the balance of power is shifting: modern platform revenue is on track to exceed traditional providers within that decade. Understanding who these processors are — and how they differ — is essential to understanding how cards actually work.

## What Does an Issuer Processor Actually Do?

An issuer processor handles everything that needs to happen in real time when a card is used — and most of what happens afterward. Here are the core functions:

**Transaction authorization.** This is the big one. When a Visa or Mastercard transaction arrives, the issuer processor receives the ISO 8583 message, evaluates it against the bank's rules, and sends back an approve or decline decision — all within milliseconds. It checks available balance, spending limits, card status, merchant category restrictions, and velocity rules (e.g., "more than 5 transactions in 10 minutes? Decline.").

**Card management.** The processor handles the full lifecycle of a card — issuing new card numbers, setting PINs, activating cards, handling replacements, managing expiration and reissuance, and tokenizing card numbers for Apple Pay and Google Pay.

**Fraud screening.** While the bank sets the fraud policies, the processor executes them in real time. This includes rules-based screening (block transactions over $5,000, decline international transactions for domestic-only cards) and increasingly, machine-learning models that score each transaction for risk.

**Ledger management.** The processor maintains the real-time balance for every card. When you spend $50, the processor immediately decrements your available balance by $50 — even before the transaction clears and settles. This is how your bank app shows you an updated balance within seconds of a purchase.

**Clearing and settlement.** At the end of each day, the processor reconciles all authorized transactions against the clearing files received from the card network, calculates net settlement amounts, and initiates the funds transfer between the bank and the network.

## The Hardware Connection: MIP and VIP

Here's something that surprises most people: connecting to Visa or Mastercard isn't a matter of calling an API. The card networks require processors to install and maintain **dedicated hardware** in certified data centers.

For Mastercard, this hardware is called a <GlossaryTerm term="MIP" definition="Mastercard Interface Processor — dedicated hardware connecting a processor to the Mastercard network (Banknet)." /> — Mastercard Interface Processor. It's a physical appliance that sits in the processor's data center and connects directly to Mastercard's Banknet network. Every authorization request for a Mastercard card flows through this hardware.

For Visa, the equivalent is <GlossaryTerm term="VIP" definition="VisaNet Integrated Payment — Visa's connection infrastructure for processor integration." /> — VisaNet Integrated Payment. Same idea: dedicated hardware connecting the processor to VisaNet.

This hardware requirement is one of the key reasons most banks don't process transactions themselves. The cost of maintaining MIP and VIP infrastructure — including the redundancy, certifications, and 24/7 operations required — runs into tens of millions of dollars. It only makes economic sense if you're processing billions of transactions a year across many clients.

## The Processor Landscape: Legacy vs. Modern

The issuer processing market splits into two distinct camps — and understanding both is critical because legacy systems still handle roughly **87% of all credit card transactions globally**. About $8 trillion flows through mainframes every single year.

### Legacy Processors

The three giants of legacy processing have been consolidating:

**TSYS (now FIS Total Issuing Solutions)** — Founded in 1959, TSYS was the largest third-party issuer processor in the world before its acquisition. It processed 35 billion transactions per year across 875 million accounts, commanding roughly 40% of North American market share. FIS acquired TSYS for $13.5 billion in January 2026, creating a combined processing powerhouse under the FIS Total Issuing Solutions brand.

**FIS** — Now owns TSYS and is going all-in on issuer processing. With the TSYS acquisition, FIS holds one of the largest card processing portfolios in the world. If you have a credit card from a top-10 US bank, there's a strong chance FIS is behind it.

**Fiserv** — The other legacy giant, serving thousands of bank clients across the US. Fiserv acquired First Data in 2019, combining issuing and acquiring processing under one roof. Fiserv's strength is its deep integration with community banks and regional institutions.

These platforms were built in an era of mainframes and batch processing. They're extraordinarily reliable — they have to be, because a processing outage means millions of cards stop working. But they're also notoriously difficult to integrate with. Getting a new card program live on a legacy processor can take 12 to 18 months. Configuration changes often require the processor's engineers to modify code on your behalf. Data access is limited — you might get batch files at the end of the day instead of real-time webhooks.

### Modern API-First Processors

Starting in the 2010s, a new generation of processors emerged, built from the ground up on modern cloud infrastructure with developer-friendly APIs. The landscape has expanded significantly, delivering what the industry calls <GlossaryTerm term="Card-as-a-Service" definition="A model where processors provide the full stack for issuing cards — from BIN sponsorship to processing — as a managed service." /> — the full stack for issuing cards as a managed, API-driven platform.

**Marqeta** — The pioneer of <GlossaryTerm term="JIT Funding" definition="Just-in-Time Funding — a model where cards hold $0 balance until a transaction occurs, funded in real-time." /> (more on that below). Marqeta processed over $383 billion in total volume through 2025 and powers Cash App, DoorDash, Instacart, and Klarna's card programs. Its open API approach made it the default choice for high-profile fintech launches.

**Galileo (SoFi)** — Acquired by SoFi for $1.2 billion in 2020, Galileo now manages over 168 million accounts. It powers Chime, Robinhood, Monzo US, and MoneyLion. Its deep account management capabilities make it the go-to for neobanks that need full banking infrastructure, not just card issuing.

**Lithic** — Born from the Privacy.com team, Lithic is laser-focused on developer experience. It offers a 1-week launch timeline — unheard of in the industry. Lithic is also one of the rare processors that connects to Visa, Mastercard, **and** American Express, which is unusual since Amex typically controls its own issuing. Powers Mercury, Novo, and numerous startups building embedded card products.

**Highnote** — The only modern processor offering unified issuing and acquiring on a single API. Uniquely, Highnote uses GraphQL rather than REST — a deliberate choice for more flexible, efficient data querying. Built entirely on Google Cloud. Named to the Forbes Fintech 50.

**i2c** — Offers over 100,000 <GlossaryTerm term="Composable Building Blocks" definition="Pre-coded, modular components that can be assembled to create custom card programs without writing code from scratch." /> — pre-coded, modular components that clients assemble to create custom card programs without writing code from scratch. Maintains 99.999% uptime (five nines). Notably, i2c runs on proprietary infrastructure — deliberately avoiding public cloud — giving it full control over its processing stack. Powers programs for Flywire, Green Dot, and numerous international clients.

**Paymentology** — Operating in 60+ countries, Paymentology is the go-to modern processor for emerging markets including Latin America, Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East. It powers M-PESA cards (the dominant mobile money platform in Africa), Agoda, and Orange. Its cloud-native, multi-region architecture is purpose-built for markets where banking infrastructure is fragmented.

**Thredd** — Strong in the UK and Europe, Thredd offers a 200-millisecond issue-and-load time for virtual cards. It powers Curve, Nium, and a growing roster of European fintech programs. Thredd's European licensing and local compliance expertise make it a natural choice for companies launching in the EU and UK.

**Enfuce** — A Finnish processor that became the first PCI-DSS certified processor running entirely in public cloud. Its 8-week onboarding timeline has made it popular with fast-moving European fintechs. Powers Pleo, the corporate expense platform.

**DECTA** — With 20+ years of processing experience, DECTA handles over 1 billion transactions per year. It holds licensed EMI (Electronic Money Institution) status in the UK, Cyprus, and Ireland — meaning it can act as both processor and issuer in certain configurations.

**BPC** — Runs the SmartVista platform, processing $52 billion across 140 countries. BPC is particularly strong in emerging markets where its modular platform lets banks and fintechs assemble payment solutions piece by piece.

### Legacy vs. Modern: A Detailed Comparison

| | Legacy Processors | Modern Processors |
|---|---|---|
| **Architecture** | Mainframe-based, batch-oriented | Cloud-native, real-time APIs |
| **Launch time** | 12–18 months | 4–12 weeks (some as fast as 1 week) |
| **Data access** | End-of-day batch files | Real-time webhooks and APIs |

| **Developer experience** | Minimal docs, relationship-driven | Developer portals, sandboxes, SDKs |

Despite every advantage listed in the right column, the legacy systems still process the overwhelming majority of global card transactions. TSYS alone handled 35 billion transactions per year before the FIS acquisition. The modern processors are growing fast — but the incumbents' installed base is enormous.

## The Evolving Role: From Bank Vendor to Platform Backbone

Historically, issuer processors existed to serve banks. A bank would select a processor like TSYS or Fiserv, sign a multi-year contract, and the processor would handle the technical plumbing while the bank owned the customer relationship, made credit decisions, and provided the capital. The processor was a back-office vendor — powerful, but invisible.

That model is changing fundamentally. Modern issuer processors increasingly serve fintechs, platforms, and consumer brands directly through APIs — often with the bank itself reduced to a regulatory wrapper in the background. The customer relationship belongs to the fintech. The product decisions belong to the platform. The processor is no longer just plumbing; it's the backbone that makes the entire program possible.

This shift has given rise to two distinct models that are important to understand: **pure-play processors** and **Credit Card as a Service (CCaaS) platforms**.

### Pure-Play Processors vs. CCaaS Providers

Pure-play processors — FIS, Fiserv, Global Payments, and the legacy TSYS infrastructure — focus exclusively on transaction processing. They handle authorization, routing, clearing, and settlement. What they don't do is provide lending capital, make credit decisions, or underwrite risk. A bank using FIS still needs its own credit team, its own capital reserves, and its own underwriting models. The processor is a pipe, not a brain.

Marqeta and Stripe Issuing fall into a similar category on the modern side — they provide excellent debit card issuing infrastructure through APIs, but they don't extend into credit issuance or underwriting. Their strength is enabling platforms to move money on prepaid and debit rails with real-time control.

CCaaS providers take a fundamentally different approach. Companies like Deserve layer credit issuance, capital provision, and underwriting on top of the processing infrastructure. Instead of handing a bank a set of APIs and saying "build your credit card program," a CCaaS provider delivers the entire credit card stack — processing, credit decisioning, capital, compliance, and program management — as a unified service.

The distinction matters because it determines who can launch a credit card program and how fast. With a pure-play processor, you still need a bank partner willing to lend, a credit team to build underwriting models, and capital to fund the portfolio. With a CCaaS provider, much of that complexity is abstracted away.

### The CCaaS Advantage: Modular, Repeatable Infrastructure

The traditional approach to launching a credit card program — even with a modern processor — often resembled a white-label model: each new customer meant significant customization, bespoke integrations, and months of configuration. Modern CCaaS platforms take a different approach, offering modular, repeatable infrastructure that can be configured rather than rebuilt for each new program.

Think of it as the difference between building a house from raw lumber every time versus assembling one from pre-engineered components. The components are standardized and tested, but they snap together in different configurations to produce distinct products. A retailer's co-branded credit card, a fintech's credit-builder product, and a platform's embedded credit offering can all run on the same underlying infrastructure — configured differently, but sharing the same reliable foundation.

This modularity is what makes it possible for non-bank platforms to enter the credit card space at all. Without it, the cost and complexity of standing up a credit card program would remain prohibitive for all but the largest institutions.

### BaaS and the Non-Bank Issuing Wave

The rise of issuer processors as platform-facing infrastructure has converged with the broader Banking-as-a-Service movement. Modern processors now integrate tightly with BaaS providers, creating a combined stack that enables non-bank platforms — retailers, SaaS companies, gig economy platforms, even airlines — to issue cards under their own brand.

On the debit and prepaid side, Marqeta and Stripe Issuing have made it straightforward for platforms to embed card issuing into their products. A marketplace can issue seller payout cards; a corporate platform can spin up virtual cards for each employee; an earned-wage-access app can give workers instant access to pay — all without the platform holding a banking license.

On the credit side, CCaaS providers extend this model to credit cards — a significantly more complex product because it involves lending, credit risk, and regulatory obligations that go far beyond moving existing money around. The integration of processing, BaaS, and credit infrastructure is what makes embedded credit card programs viable for the first time.

## Geographic Coverage: Where Processors Dominate

Processor choice often depends as much on geography as on technology. The market breaks down regionally:

**US-dominated:** Marqeta, Galileo, Lithic, Highnote, and TSYS/FIS are strongest in North America. If you're launching a card program in the US, these are your primary options.

**Europe-focused:** Thredd, Enfuce, and DECTA are built for the European regulatory landscape — PSD2 compliance, Strong Customer Authentication, and multi-currency support across the Eurozone and UK.

**Emerging markets:** Paymentology and BPC specialize in regions where banking infrastructure is less mature — Latin America, Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East. Their platforms are designed for markets with fragmented banking systems and high mobile-money adoption.

**Global reach:** i2c and Fiserv operate across multiple continents, making them natural choices for programs that need to launch in multiple geographies simultaneously.

If you're a fintech planning to issue cards in the UK and then expand to Nigeria, you'd likely work with Paymentology or Thredd rather than Marqeta — not because of technology quality, but because of licensing, network certifications, and local compliance expertise.

## API Design Philosophy

How a processor exposes its capabilities matters more than most fintechs realize when evaluating partners.

**REST APIs** are the standard for most modern processors. Marqeta, Galileo, Lithic, and Paymentology all offer RESTful endpoints for card creation, transaction management, and account operations. REST is well-understood, widely supported, and easy to integrate with.

**GraphQL** is the outlier approach, used by Highnote. GraphQL lets clients request exactly the data they need in a single query — no over-fetching, no under-fetching. For card programs that need complex, nested data (a cardholder's transactions, grouped by merchant category, filtered by date range, with dispute status), GraphQL can be significantly more efficient than multiple REST calls.

**Legacy protocols** are still the backbone of traditional processing. ISO 8583 message formats, batch files delivered via SFTP, and proprietary data formats remain the standard for TSYS/FIS, Fiserv, and their bank clients. These protocols are battle-tested but require specialized knowledge to implement and maintain.

**Webhooks and event streaming** are increasingly critical. Modern processors push real-time events — transaction authorized, card activated, dispute filed — to the fintech's systems via webhooks. This is what makes features like instant transaction notifications and real-time spending analytics possible. Legacy processors typically deliver this data hours later in batch files.

## The JIT Funding Innovation

One of the most important innovations in modern issuer processing is <GlossaryTerm term="JIT Funding" definition="Just-in-Time Funding — a model where cards hold $0 balance until a transaction occurs, funded in real-time." /> — Just-in-Time Funding, pioneered by Marqeta.

In the traditional model, a prepaid or debit card holds a balance. When you load $500 onto your card, those funds sit with the processor or the bank, waiting to be spent. This creates a problem for fintechs: they need to pre-fund pools of money that sit idle until customers spend it.

JIT Funding flips this model. The card holds a **$0 balance**. When a transaction comes in, the processor sends a real-time webhook to the fintech's system: "Your customer is trying to spend $47.50 at Target. Do you approve?" The fintech's system checks its own ledger, decides whether to approve, and if yes, funds the transaction in real time. The card's balance goes from $0 to $47.50 to $0 again, all in milliseconds.

This creates two variants:

- **Gateway JIT:** The processor sends every transaction to the fintech for a real-time decision. The fintech has full control — it can apply custom business logic, check its own balance systems, and approve or decline based on its own rules. DoorDash uses this for its Dasher pay cards, applying custom rules about when drivers can access earnings.
- **Managed JIT:** The processor handles the authorization decision based on pre-configured rules, but still funds just-in-time from the fintech's pooled account. Simpler to implement, less custom control.

JIT Funding is what makes modern fintech card products possible. It's why Chime can offer fee-free overdraft (SpotMe), why Cash App can give you instant access to deposits, and why corporate expense cards can enforce per-merchant spending limits in real time.

## The BaaS Stack: How Fintechs Launch Card Programs

If you're a fintech that wants to issue cards — say you're building an expense management tool and want to give each employee a virtual Visa card — you can't just call up Visa and ask for cards. You need an entire stack of partners. - **<GlossaryTerm term="Sponsor Bank" definition="A chartered bank that provides its banking license and BIN to enable fintechs to issue cards on card networks." />** — A chartered bank (like Sutton Bank, Celtic Bank, or Bancorp Bank) that provides its banking license and BIN (Bank Identification Number). Legally, they're the issuer. In practice, they're providing regulatory cover.
- **Issuer Processor** — The technology platform (Marqeta, Galileo, etc.) that handles authorization, card management, and settlement.
- **<GlossaryTerm term="Program Manager" definition="The entity that manages day-to-day operations of a card program — compliance, customer service, vendor coordination." />** — The entity that manages the day-to-day operations: compliance monitoring, customer service, vendor coordination, regulatory reporting. Sometimes the fintech itself acts as program manager; sometimes a third party like Deserve or Highnote fills this role.
- **KYC / Identity Provider** — Handles customer identity verification (Alloy, Socure, Persona).

This is why fintech card programs list a bank name in the fine print that you've never heard of. When your Chime card says "Bancorp Bank" on it, that's the sponsor bank. Chime is the brand. Galileo is the processor. They all work together, but the processor is the central nervous system.

## The Business Model

How do issuer processors make money? Several ways:

**Per-transaction fees.** The processor charges a small fee — typically $0.03 to $0.10 — for each authorization, whether approved or declined. At scale, this adds up fast. A processor handling 10 billion transactions per year at $0.05 each generates $500 million in transaction fees alone.

**Platform fees.** Monthly per-account fees for card management, typically $0.50 to $2.00 per active account.

**Interchange sharing.** Some modern processors negotiate a share of the interchange fee that the issuing bank earns. This is especially common in fintech programs where the processor's technology is central to the program's value.

**Card manufacturing and fulfillment.** Physical card production, personalization, and mailing.

## Why Do Banks Outsource This?

Given how central processing is, you might wonder: why don't banks just do it themselves? The largest banks — like JPMorgan Chase — do run their own processing for some products. But the vast majority of banks and all fintechs outsource, for compelling reasons:

**Infrastructure cost.** Maintaining MIP/VIP hardware, redundant data centers, and 24/7 operations is enormously expensive. It only makes sense at massive scale.

**Compliance burden.** Processors must maintain PCI DSS Level 1 certification, pass network audits, and meet stringent uptime requirements. This is a specialized competency.

**Speed to market.** A bank launching a new card product on its own infrastructure might take two years. Using a modern processor, it can launch in weeks.

**Innovation.** Modern processors invest hundreds of millions in R&D — real-time webhooks, tokenization, virtual card issuance, JIT Funding. Most banks can't match this pace of innovation on their own.

**Scalability.** Processors are built to handle dramatic swings in volume — Black Friday, tax refund season, stimulus payments. This elasticity is hard to build in-house.

The issuer processor might be the most important company in the payments ecosystem that most people have never heard of. Every card in your wallet, every fintech app on your phone, every "Approved" on a terminal screen — there's a processor making it happen. And as the market shifts from legacy mainframes to modern APIs, the companies that power the next generation of card programs will shape how billions of people interact with money.
