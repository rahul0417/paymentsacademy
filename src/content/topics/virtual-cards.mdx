## A Card Without the Card

You know what a card number looks like. Sixteen digits, an expiration date, a CVV on the back. Now imagine generating one of those instantly — through an API call — with no plastic involved. No waiting for the mail. No activating a physical card. Just a number, ready to use in milliseconds.

That's a <GlossaryTerm term="Virtual Card" definition="A digital-only card number generated instantly, usable for online purchases or added to mobile wallets — no physical plastic needed.">virtual card</GlossaryTerm>. It has all the components of a traditional payment card — a 16-digit PAN, expiration date, and CVV — but it exists only as data. It can be used for online purchases, added to Apple Pay or Google Pay for in-store tap payments, or embedded directly into software workflows. No plastic, no embossing, no postage.

Virtual cards aren't new. Banks have offered "virtual card numbers" for years. What's new is programmability. Modern virtual cards aren't just digital versions of plastic — they're programmable spending instruments with built-in controls, limits, and lifecycle rules. And they're being generated by the billions.

## How Virtual Cards Are Issued

The mechanics are straightforward. A company partners with an issuer processor — Marqeta, Lithic, Highnote, or similar — and a sponsor bank that holds the actual banking license. The issuer processor provides the technology platform, and the sponsor bank provides the <GlossaryTerm term="BIN Range" definition="A block of card numbers assigned to an issuer, used to generate virtual cards programmatically.">BIN range</GlossaryTerm> — the block of card numbers allocated by Visa or Mastercard.

When the company needs a new virtual card, it makes an API call. The processor generates a unique card number from the BIN range, assigns an expiry and CVV, and returns the full card credentials in the API response. The entire process takes milliseconds.

Compare this to physical card issuance. A plastic card requires manufacturing, personalization (printing the cardholder's name and number), packaging, and shipping. That takes 5-10 business days at best. Virtual cards eliminate every step of that process.

## Single-Use vs Multi-Use

Virtual cards come in two fundamental flavors, and the distinction matters.

A <GlossaryTerm term="Single-Use Card" definition="A virtual card that's valid for one transaction only, then automatically deactivated — maximizing security.">single-use card</GlossaryTerm> is generated for exactly one transaction. Once it's been charged, the card number automatically deactivates. Even if the number is compromised, it's worthless — it can't be charged again. Single-use cards are the gold standard for security-sensitive purchases, one-time supplier payments, and any scenario where you want zero risk of unauthorized follow-up charges.

Multi-use virtual cards persist across multiple transactions. They function like a regular card but with programmable controls. You might issue a multi-use virtual card to an employee for recurring software subscriptions, with a $500/month spending limit and restrictions to only work at SaaS merchants. The card stays active until you deactivate it.

The ability to choose — and to switch between these modes programmatically — is what makes virtual cards fundamentally different from plastic. A physical card is always multi-use. You can't make it self-destruct after one purchase.

## The Use Cases

Virtual cards have found product-market fit across a surprisingly wide range of scenarios.

**Corporate expense management.** This is the biggest use case by volume. Companies like Brex, Ramp, and Divvy issue unique virtual cards for every expense category, department, or even individual purchase. An engineering team gets a virtual card for AWS. Marketing gets one for Google Ads. Each employee gets their own card for travel. Every card has its own spending limit, merchant category restrictions, and approval workflows. When someone leaves the company, their cards are deactivated instantly — no need to cancel a shared corporate card and reissue to everyone else.

**B2B supplier payments.** When a company needs to pay an invoice to a supplier, it can generate a single-use virtual card for the exact invoice amount. The supplier charges the card, and it's done. The buyer earns interchange revenue on the payment (yes, the buyer earns money by paying this way), and the supplier gets paid faster than waiting for a check or ACH. Companies like Nvoicepay and Corpay have built entire businesses around this model.

**Rewards and incentives.** Need to send a $100 prize to a contest winner instantly? Generate a virtual card, email or text them the number, and they can spend it immediately. No mailing gift cards. No waiting. Insurance companies use this for instant claim payouts. Employers use it for spot bonuses. Market research firms use it to compensate survey participants.

**QA and testing.** Software teams need to test payment flows constantly. Virtual cards let QA engineers generate fresh card numbers for every test case — different amounts, different card types, different decline scenarios. No sharing a single test card across the team. No worrying about hitting transaction limits on a test card.

**BNPL checkout.** Buy Now, Pay Later 2.0 providers use single-use virtual cards to enable in-app shopping experiences. The consumer browses and selects items within the BNPL app, and when they're ready to check out, the BNPL provider generates a virtual card loaded with the exact purchase amount. The consumer uses that card to complete the transaction at the merchant — the merchant sees a normal card payment, while the consumer pays the BNPL provider back in installments. This model lets BNPL providers work at any merchant that accepts cards, without requiring direct merchant integrations.

**Embedded finance card issuing.** Virtual card issuing has become a key embedded finance product. Software platforms — from vertical SaaS companies to marketplaces — can now issue cards to their users and earn interchange revenue on every transaction. Stripe Issuing and Marqeta provide the infrastructure that makes this possible at scale, handling the card network integrations, compliance, and transaction processing. For the platform, each card swipe generates revenue without the user ever leaving the platform's ecosystem.

**Subscription management.** Here's a clever consumer use case: assign a unique virtual card to each subscription service. Netflix gets one card number. Spotify gets another. Your gym gets a third. If you want to cancel a subscription that makes cancellation difficult, just deactivate that specific virtual card. The charge declines, and the service can't bill you. More importantly, if one service has a data breach, only that one card number is compromised — not the card you use everywhere.

## Security: The Built-In Advantage

Virtual cards are inherently more secure than physical cards, and it's not even close.

**Programmable spending limits.** Set a maximum of $50, $500, or $5,000. The card physically cannot be charged above that amount — the authorization will be declined by the issuer processor in real time.

**Merchant restrictions.** Lock a card to specific merchant category codes (MCCs). An employee's travel card works at airlines and hotels but declines at electronics stores. A marketing card works at advertising platforms but not at restaurants.

**Time windows.** Create a card that's only valid for 24 hours, or for the month of March, or until a specific project ends. After the window closes, the card automatically deactivates.

**Geographic restrictions.** Limit a card to transactions in specific countries. A domestic procurement card that can't be used internationally eliminates an entire category of fraud risk.

**Instant deactivation.** Suspect fraud? Deactivate the card via API in milliseconds. No calling a bank. No waiting on hold. No 7-10 business days for a replacement card. Just generate a new virtual card and move on.

All of these controls are enforced at authorization time — the moment a transaction is attempted. The issuer processor evaluates the card's rules and approves or declines in real time. This is fundamentally different from post-transaction expense policies, where violations are discovered during monthly reviews and then clawed back.

## Mobile Wallet Provisioning

Virtual cards aren't limited to online purchases. Through mobile wallet provisioning, a virtual card can be pushed directly to a cardholder's Apple Pay, Google Pay, or Samsung Pay wallet — enabling in-store tap-to-pay at any NFC terminal.

The flow works like this: the application generates a virtual card, then initiates a push provisioning request through the card network's tokenization service. The card network issues a device-bound token (DPAN), which appears in the cardholder's mobile wallet. From the cardholder's perspective, a new card simply appears in their wallet app, ready to tap.

This is how companies like Brex onboard new employees. Day one, the employee downloads the Brex app, and their corporate virtual card is pushed to their Apple Pay. They can buy lunch on their first day without waiting for plastic to arrive. That's a meaningful improvement in employee experience — and it eliminates the risk of the physical card being lost in the mail.

## The Economics

Virtual cards ride on the same card network rails as physical cards. When a virtual Visa card is charged, Visa processes it exactly like any other Visa transaction. Interchange flows to the issuer, network assessments flow to Visa, and the merchant pays the same acceptance fees.

But virtual cards shift who captures the interchange. In the B2B supplier payment use case, the buying company earns interchange on payments they'd otherwise make via check or ACH (which generate no interchange). For a company paying $10 million per month to suppliers via virtual card at an average interchange rate of 1.5%, that's $150,000 per month in interchange revenue — revenue that didn't exist before. This is why AP automation companies aggressively push virtual card adoption.

The issuer processor typically charges the program manager (Brex, Ramp, etc.) a per-card issuance fee and a per-transaction fee. These are small — fractions of a cent for card creation, pennies per transaction. The program manager keeps the lion's share of interchange as its revenue.

## Why Now?

Virtual cards have existed for over a decade, so why the explosion in adoption? Three things changed simultaneously.

First, modern issuer processors like Marqeta and Lithic built developer-friendly APIs that make card issuance as easy as a REST call. The old model — working directly with a bank's card management system through COBOL mainframe integrations — was so painful that only large companies attempted it. APIs democratized card issuance.

Second, the pandemic accelerated the shift from physical to digital payments. When offices closed and employees were remote, mailing physical corporate cards became impractical. Virtual cards solved the distribution problem instantly.

Third, the corporate spend management market matured. Brex (founded 2017), Ramp (founded 2019), and their competitors proved that virtual cards combined with software controls could replace traditional corporate cards and expense management tools. Their growth has been staggering — Ramp reached a $100 million revenue run rate faster than nearly any other SaaS company in history.

Virtual card issuance is now growing at 20-30% annually. By some estimates, virtual cards will account for the majority of new commercial card issuance within the next few years. The physical corporate card isn't dead yet, but for most use cases, it's hard to argue why you'd wait a week for plastic when you can have a card in milliseconds.
